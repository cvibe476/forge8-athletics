What a Hyrox race asks of you

A Hyrox is eight 1,000-meter runs with eight functional stations between them. The movements are simple and you have seen every one of them before. What makes the race hard is the order: each station arrives on legs that already have a kilometre in them, and the next run starts before your breathing has settled.

That is why the training looks less like a series of hard workouts and more like a running plan with strength bolted to it. You need an aerobic base that holds up across eight efforts, enough strength to move a sled and a sandbag when you are already tired, and the pacing discipline to stop the first two kilometres from costing you the last two.

None of that is exotic. It is coached, repeatable work, done often enough to stick.

The eight stations

These are the movements the race will ask for, in race order, with a 1,000-meter run between each one:

  1. 1,000-meter ski erg
  2. 50-meter sled push
  3. 50-meter sled pull
  4. 80-meter burpee broad jumps
  5. 1,000-meter row
  6. 200-meter farmers carry
  7. 100-meter sandbag lunges
  8. 100 wall balls

Loads and rep counts are set by division, so the same station can mean a very different thing depending on which category you enter. We train the movement patterns and the pacing, and you confirm the numbers against your own division before race day.

How training works here

Hyrox work runs inside the regular schedule, not as a separate membership. Thursday classes blend CrossFit and Hyrox-specific work — some weeks on sled mechanics and station transitions, others on running volume or the strength that keeps your positions together late in a race.

The rest of the week supports it. Regular CrossFit classes build the broad base — squatting, pulling, carrying, conditioning — that a race exposes the gaps in. The athlete who only practises stations tends to arrive fit but narrow. The one who trains both arrives able to hold form at kilometre seven.

Unlimited members also have 24/7 open gym access, which is where a lot of real race preparation happens: easy aerobic volume that does not fit a class slot, a few rounds of wall balls, or sled work after a session.

Every class is coached and every session is scaled. If you have never touched a sled, that is a normal starting point here — not a problem to work around.

Training for a race on the calendar

Most people arrive here with a date already picked. What changes between eight weeks out and two weeks out is not the amount of work — it is what the work is for.

Far out, build the base. Running volume, general strength, and enough consistency that the sessions stack. A race is eight kilometres of running; the athlete who cannot run comfortably cannot be saved by station skill. This is most of a training block and it is deliberately unglamorous.

Closer in, rehearse the race. Station practice at race pace, transitions between them, and a plan for how you will break up the rep counts. Wall balls at rep 80 feel different from wall balls at rep 20, and the only way to learn your split is to get there in training.

Two weeks out, taper. Volume comes down, intensity stays short and sharp, and you stop trying to add fitness you did not build earlier in the block. Arriving fresh is worth more than one more hard session.

If you are training for something else — a first race, a mountain, a military fitness test — the same shape applies. Coached classes give you the strength and conditioning half; open gym gives you the space to add the specific half.

What to expect on a Thursday

Sessions run inside the regular class hour, alongside everyone else on the floor. A typical Thursday moves between three kinds of work:

  • Running at a pace you can repeat, not a pace you can survive once
  • Station mechanics — sled body angle and step length, erg sequencing, sandbag position on the lunge, wall ball rhythm
  • Conditioning that puts a station after a run, which is the only way to practise the part that actually hurts

Everything scales. If you have never used a ski erg, that is what the first session is for. Coaches will set your load and your distance to match where you are, then move both as you adapt.
