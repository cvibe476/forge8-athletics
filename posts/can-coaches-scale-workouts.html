Can Coaches Scale Workouts? Yes, and Here’s How

2026-09-11 · CrossFit Forge8 Athletics · Miramar, San Diego

A barbell loaded for one athlete can be the wrong tool for the person standing right beside them. That does not mean either person is in the wrong class. Can coaches scale workouts without watering down the training? Absolutely. In a well-coached functional fitness class, scaling is how every athlete trains the intended stimulus safely, builds skill, and leaves stronger than when they walked in.

At Forge8, nobody is expected to figure it out alone. The workout has a purpose. The coach helps you find the version that matches your current ability, your training history, and how you are moving that day. You still work. You still earn the result. You just train the right version of the work.

Can Coaches Scale Workouts Without Making Them Easier?

Yes. Scaling does not mean making a workout easy. It means adjusting the workout so that the effort, movement pattern, and intended training effect remain intact.

Take a workout with heavy deadlifts and a short run. An experienced athlete may use a heavier barbell and run the prescribed distance at a fast pace. A newer athlete may use a lighter barbell, elevate the starting position, or reduce the load so every rep stays safe and consistent. If running is not appropriate because of an injury or impact tolerance, the coach may substitute a bike, rower, or another conditioning option that keeps the workout moving.

Both athletes are training strength under fatigue. Both are working on posterior-chain strength, breathing control, and pacing. Both should finish challenged. The details change, but the point of the workout does not.

That distinction matters. Randomly cutting reps or choosing a lighter weight because it feels comfortable is not smart scaling. It can remove the very challenge that creates adaptation. Good coaching finds the line between an athlete who is being pushed and an athlete who is being set up to fail.

What a Coach Looks at Before Scaling

A coach does not scale workouts from a chart alone. Percentages and standard substitutions can help, but the athlete in front of them matters more. The right option depends on movement quality, experience, current fitness, recovery, and the goal of that day’s session.

For a beginner, the first priority may be learning positions. If the workout includes overhead squats, a coach may start with a PVC pipe, an empty bar, a front squat variation, or a squat to a target. The athlete is not skipping the workout. They are building the mobility, control, and confidence needed to earn more range of motion and more load over time.

For an experienced athlete, scaling may look different. Maybe they are returning after travel, carrying fatigue from a hard Hyrox training session, or protecting a shoulder that is not ready for high-volume kipping. They may keep the intensity but change the movement, control the weight, or set a pace that supports recovery. Smart athletes do this all the time. They train for the long game, not just the scoreboard for one day.

A coach also watches what happens once the clock starts. A plan made during the whiteboard brief might need to change after the first round. If technique breaks down, rest gets too long, or an athlete is redlining far beyond the intended effort, the coach can adjust in real time. That hands-on attention is one reason coached classes beat guessing your way through a crowded gym.

The Main Ways Coaches Scale a Workout

Most workout scaling falls into a few practical categories. The coach may change the load, the volume, the movement, the range of motion, or the time domain. Often, the best solution uses more than one.

Load and Volume

Load is the most familiar adjustment. A lighter kettlebell, dumbbell, barbell, or medicine ball can make a movement repeatable without turning it into a grind. The right load allows the athlete to hold solid positions and complete quality sets rather than surviving every rep.

Volume matters just as much. A workout written with 50 pull-ups may be appropriate for an athlete who can complete them in controlled sets. For someone developing that capacity, 20 or 30 well-executed reps can deliver a better training result than 50 rushed reps with torn hands and failing mechanics.

The question is not, Can you technically finish the prescribed workout? The better question is, Can you complete it with movement that builds you instead of breaking you down?

Movement Substitutions

Some movements require progressions. A pull-up can become a banded pull-up, jumping pull-up, ring row, or strict pulling variation depending on the athlete’s strength and skill. A handstand push-up can become a seated dumbbell press, pike push-up, or elevated pressing variation. Box jumps can become step-ups when landing mechanics, confidence, or an irritated Achilles call for a different choice.

A good substitute preserves as much of the original demand as possible. If the workout is built around vertical pulling, a coach chooses another vertical or closely related pulling pattern when possible. If the workout calls for explosive hip extension, the replacement should still train power or a comparable conditioning demand.

This is why substitutions should not be random. Burpees and biceps curls are both exercises, but they are not interchangeable. The movement must fit the training goal.

Range of Motion and Skill Progressions

Full range of motion is a standard worth pursuing, but forcing it before an athlete can control it is not. Scaling range can help athletes build toward the full movement without reinforcing poor habits.

That might mean squatting to a box, deadlifting from raised plates, using a target for wall balls, or reducing the depth of a push-up while maintaining a strong plank. As mobility, stability, and strength improve, the progression changes. What begins as a modification becomes a bridge to the full movement.

There is no prize for pretending a limitation does not exist. There is progress in addressing it with consistency.

Pace and Time Caps

Some workouts are designed to feel like a sprint. Others are built for longer, sustainable conditioning. If an athlete takes twice as long as the intended time domain, the workout can become something else entirely.

A coach may reduce rounds, shorten a run, or adjust reps so the athlete stays inside the right window. This protects the stimulus and keeps class training focused. It also teaches pacing - a critical skill for CrossFit workouts, Hyrox race preparation, and any athlete who wants to stop fading halfway through a hard effort.

Scaling in a Group Class Takes Real Coaching

A common concern is whether one coach can serve beginners and advanced athletes in the same class. The answer depends on how the class is run. A coach who only reads the workout off a board cannot provide meaningful individualization. A coach who demonstrates movement standards, asks about injuries, watches warm-up reps, and stays engaged through the workout can.

The best group environment gives you structure without treating everyone like the same person. You train alongside people who may be stronger, faster, newer, or preparing for a race, but your plan is built for you. That shared effort creates accountability without comparison becoming the whole point.

For busy adults in Miramar and across San Diego, this is practical. You do not need to spend an hour building a program, researching substitutions, or wondering whether you chose the right weight. Show up, communicate honestly, and let the coach guide the session.

How to Help Your Coach Scale You Well

Coaching works best when athletes speak up early. Tell your coach about a past injury, a movement that feels off, a recent race, poor sleep, or a goal you are training toward. None of that is an excuse. It is useful information.

Arrive with enough time to hear the workout brief and take the warm-up seriously. This is where a coach can see your mobility, check positions, and identify the right progression before intensity enters the picture. If you are unsure about a weight, ask. Starting slightly too light and building is usually better than loading up and spending the workout fighting ugly reps.

Then stay open to feedback. Being coached is a strength. The athlete who adjusts, learns, and returns tomorrow will outlast the athlete who treats every session like a test.

Scaling Builds More Than a Single Workout

The real value of scaling is consistency. It lets a complete beginner start now instead of waiting until they feel ready. It lets a competitive athlete manage hard training without digging a recovery hole. It lets someone returning from injury rebuild with purpose rather than fear.

Over weeks and months, those right-sized sessions stack up. The lighter barbell becomes a heavier one. The box-supported squat becomes a full-depth squat. The ring row becomes a pull-up. Progress is forged through repeatable work, not reckless effort.

Come in ready to work, be honest about where you are, and let a coach meet you there. The right workout is not the one that looks hardest from across the room. It is the one that pushes you further and gives you the capacity to come back stronger tomorrow.
