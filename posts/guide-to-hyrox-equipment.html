HYROX Equipment Guide: Train Race-Ready in San Diego | Forge8

2026-10-15 · CrossFit Forge8 Athletics · Miramar, San Diego

HYROX is not a race you can fake with a few hard treadmill sessions. You need to run under fatigue, move awkward loads with control, and keep working when your legs are already full. This guide to Hyrox equipment shows you what each station demands, what to practice, and what gear actually earns a place in your training bag.

The good news: you do not need to build a warehouse gym to prepare. Race equipment is supplied on event day. Your job is to get familiar with the movement patterns, build capacity, and learn how to stay composed when the work gets heavy.

The Eight Pieces of Hyrox Equipment

A standard HYROX race alternates eight 1,000-meter runs with eight functional workout stations. The order matters because every station changes how your next run feels. Training each piece in isolation is useful. Training the transitions is where race readiness gets forged.

SkiErg

The SkiErg opens the race. It looks simple until you pull hard enough to raise your heart rate before the first real running block is underway. The movement uses your lats, core, hips, and triceps, but the best athletes do not turn it into an arm-only battle.

Drive down with the whole body, then return under control. Keep your shoulders from creeping toward your ears. If you are new to the machine, start with steady intervals and technique work before chasing all-out power. A fast opening split is worthless if it wrecks your breathing for the next run.

Sled Push

The sled push is where race plans get honest. Load, turf friction, body position, and fatigue all matter. In a HYROX event, athletes drive a loaded sled down and back over a set distance. The weight changes by division, so check the current race standards for your category before setting training loads.

Use a forward body angle, locked-out arms, and short, forceful steps. Do not stand upright and try to walk the sled forward. Get low, push through the floor, and keep the sled moving. At Forge8, coaches can help you find a load that challenges you without turning every training session into a grind that compromises recovery.

Sled Pull

The sled pull asks for a different kind of strength. You need a strong grip, braced trunk, and the patience to pull hand over hand without yanking yourself out of position. Many athletes burn through their forearms because they pull too frantically in the first few meters.

Sit back into a stable stance, keep tension through the rope, and use your legs as much as possible. Pull in a repeatable rhythm. If your gym does not have a race-style rope and sled setup, heavy cable pulls, rope pulls, and backward drags can build useful strength. They are substitutes, not exact replacements, so get time on the real setup when you can.

Burpee Broad Jumps

Burpee broad jumps require no fancy machine, which is exactly why people underestimate them. The problem is not one rep. It is repeating a burpee, a forward jump, and a reset while trying to control your breathing.

Stay disciplined. Step down and step up if that keeps your form steady and your heart rate manageable. Use a consistent jump distance instead of trying to launch yourself as far as possible on every rep. Smaller, repeatable jumps usually beat a few heroic leaps followed by a long recovery.

RowErg

The rower is another full-body station, but it should not become a back-and-biceps workout. Legs initiate the drive. Your torso follows. Your arms finish. On the return, reverse the sequence and let the handle come back smoothly.

A powerful stroke rate is not always a high stroke rate. Find a pace that lets you produce force without spiking your heart rate beyond control. Since the row comes after burpee broad jumps, train it with tired legs sometimes. That is the version you will meet on race day.

Farmer's Carry

For the farmer's carry, athletes carry two heavy kettlebells over the prescribed distance. Grip is the headline, but posture is the real test. If your ribs flare, shoulders round, and steps get sloppy, every meter becomes more expensive.

Stand tall, squeeze the handles, take short controlled steps, and resist leaning side to side. Practice with kettlebells, dumbbells, or farmer handles. Heavy holds and loaded carries are valuable additions, especially for athletes whose hands quit before their lungs do.

Sandbag Lunges

Sandbag lunges punish poor bracing. The bag sits across your shoulders while you lunge forward for distance, often when your legs are already cooked. The load shifts, your balance changes, and rushed reps can turn ugly quickly.

Keep the bag secure, take a controlled step, and lower your back knee toward the floor with your torso tall. Choose a lunge variation you can own in training before adding load. Beginners can build toward the movement with bodyweight reverse lunges, goblet lunges, and lighter sandbags. Scaling is not a shortcut. It is how you build durable mechanics.

Wall Balls

Wall balls close the race, and they demand more than tired legs. You need to squat to a consistent depth, drive the ball to the required target, catch cleanly, and repeat without losing count or composure.

Use your legs to send the ball up. Catch it softly, descend with control, and break reps before your form falls apart. Sets of 10 may be smarter than a reckless set of 30. The right break strategy depends on your conditioning and division, but standing frozen under the target never helps.

What You Need to Bring on Race Day

The race organizer provides the stations, sleds, kettlebells, sandbags, wall balls, and cardio machines. You do not need to bring your own equipment. You do need gear that lets you move well across running, turf work, and loaded stations.

Running shoes with reliable traction are the priority. Choose a pair you have already tested on short runs, turns, burpees, lunges, and sled work. A pure racing shoe may feel fast on pavement but unstable under a heavy sled. A bulky training shoe may feel secure on turf but slow and uncomfortable through eight kilometers. There is no universal winner. Test your own race-day option under fatigue.

Wear clothing that stays put when you squat, lunge, and pull a rope. Bring water, simple fuel you have used before, a towel, and dry layers for after the finish. Gloves are a personal choice. They may protect your hands, but they can also reduce your feel on the rope or kettlebell handle. Do not make race day the first time you try them.

Build a Smarter Hyrox Equipment Plan

The best HYROX preparation is not eight stations thrown together at maximum effort every week. That approach creates soreness, poor movement, and inconsistent training. Build strength for the loaded work, aerobic capacity for the running, and station skill for efficiency.

Start by identifying your limiting factor. If running is your weakness, the sled will not fix it. If grip fails during carries and pulls, add loaded carries and pulling work. If wall balls fall apart, improve your squat endurance and practice target accuracy. Honest assessment saves time.

Then rehearse combinations. Pair a 1,000-meter run with a station-specific interval. Practice sled push into an easy run, or burpee broad jumps into rowing. As your base improves, build toward longer race-style sessions. Keep at least some training days focused on quality strength and controlled conditioning rather than constant simulation.

If you are starting from scratch, do not wait until you feel "ready" to get coached. A good program meets you where you are, scales the load, and gives you a clear next step. HYROX rewards people who show up consistently, not just people who can suffer through one brutal workout.

Your equipment is only part of the equation. Learn the stations, build the engine, respect the transitions, and train with people who will push you further without leaving you to figure it out alone. Show up prepared. Then go earn every meter.
