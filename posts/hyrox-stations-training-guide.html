Hyrox Stations Training Guide for Race Day

2026-08-13 · CrossFit Forge8 Athletics · Miramar, San Diego

A Hyrox race is not won by the athlete with the best single station. It is won by the athlete who can run hard, settle their breathing, execute under fatigue, and move on without falling apart. This Hyrox stations training guide gives you a practical way to prepare for all eight stations while building the engine to repeat that effort eight times.

The work is simple. The preparation is not. You need strength for the sleds and lunges, skill for wall balls and burpees, grip for carries, and enough aerobic control to make the next 1,000-meter run feel possible. Train each piece with purpose. Then train the transitions between them.

What Hyrox Training Actually Demands

Hyrox combines eight 1,000-meter runs with eight functional stations: SkiErg, sled push, sled pull, burpee broad jumps, rowing, farmer's carry, sandbag lunges, and wall balls. That format creates a different challenge than a standard gym workout. You cannot treat the stations like isolated strength tests, because every station arrives after a run and every station affects the run that follows.

Your goal is not to redline early. Your goal is to hold a repeatable output. That may mean using a lighter sled in training while dialing in body position, or breaking wall balls into smaller sets before your shoulders are smoked. Race preparation is built through controlled, repeatable work - not random suffering.

For beginners, scaled loads and shorter intervals are the right starting point. For experienced competitors, the focus shifts toward race-weight exposure, pacing discipline, and station efficiency. Both athletes need coaching, consistency, and a plan.

Hyrox Stations Training Guide: Train Each Weak Link

SkiErg: Build Power Without Blowing Up

The SkiErg looks like an upper-body station, but strong reps come from a full-body hinge. Start tall, drive your hands down, hinge through the hips, then finish with your arms. If you pull only with your shoulders, your arms will burn long before the monitor shows 1,000 meters.

Train both smooth aerobic efforts and shorter hard intervals. One day, hold a steady pace for 750 to 1,000 meters. Another day, complete intervals such as 10 rounds of 45 seconds hard and 45 seconds easy. Focus on long, powerful strokes instead of frantic pulling. The best pace is the one that lets you run immediately afterward.

Sled Push: Own Your Body Position

The sled push is where race plans get honest. Heavy legs, a forward lean, and a loaded sled can turn a short lane into a long fight. Your setup matters: arms locked, torso angled forward, ribs braced, and feet driving through the ground in short, forceful steps.

Build this station with heavy, controlled pushes and moderate-load repeat efforts. Heavy work develops the force needed to move the sled. Lighter, longer pushes teach you to keep moving when your legs are full of acid. If your gym surface or sled friction differs from race conditions, do not obsess over comparing exact weight. Use training to improve position and sustained drive.

Sled Pull: Pull With Your Whole Frame

The sled pull punishes athletes who try to win with their biceps. Stay low, lean back, brace your trunk, and use hand-over-hand pulls while stepping backward with control. Keep the rope organized. A tangled rope or rushed turn costs energy you do not get back.

Practice pulling a moderate sled for repeated lengths, then add a short run. The station is as much about rhythm as brute strength. Pull, step, reset. Do not let your hips drift forward and your chest collapse.

Burpee Broad Jumps: Stay Low and Keep Moving

Burpee broad jumps are rarely elegant late in a race. That is fine. They need to be efficient. Drop under control, get your chest to the floor, step or jump your feet in, and use a small, repeatable broad jump. Huge jumps feel fast for three reps and expensive for 30.

Use a consistent turnaround method in training. Land, pivot, and drop. Avoid standing fully upright between reps unless you need a brief reset. If you are new to burpees, reduce the total distance or use step-back variations first. Build durable movement before chasing speed.

Rowing: Settle Down, Then Work

The row is a chance to regain control, but only if you use it well. Drive with the legs first, then swing the torso, then finish with the arms. On the recovery, reverse the sequence. Rushing the slide wastes energy and spikes your heart rate.

Aim for a sustainable stroke rate rather than trying to yank every stroke as hard as possible. If you can hold a strong pace while breathing under control, you will leave the rower ready to run. If you empty the tank chasing a few seconds, the next kilometer will collect the debt.

Farmer's Carry: Train Grip and Posture

The farmer's carry is simple until your forearms are cooked and your core starts folding. Pick up the kettlebells with a braced trunk, stand tall, and take short, deliberate steps. Do not shrug your shoulders toward your ears. Keep them packed and let your grip do its job.

Heavy carries build confidence, while longer moderate carries build race durability. Add carries after pulling, rowing, or deadlift work to practice holding on when your grip is already taxed. If you need to put the bells down, do it deliberately. One quick reset is better than multiple panicked drops.

Sandbag Lunges: Protect Your Midline

Sandbag lunges demand leg strength, balance, and control when fatigue is high. Secure the bag across your shoulders or upper back according to the race standard, brace hard, and take consistent steps. Your front foot should land flat enough to create a stable base before you drive up.

Train lunges with quality first. A long unbroken set is not useful if your knee caves inward or your torso folds. Build from short sets with clean mechanics to longer sets at race load. Walking lunges after running are especially valuable because they teach you how to stay organized on tired legs.

Wall Balls: Have a Break Plan Before You Need One

Wall balls close the race for a reason. They expose every rushed decision you made earlier. Use a squat that is controlled and repeatable, keep your chest tall, and catch the ball close to your body. Throwing it too high or catching it with straight arms creates unnecessary fatigue.

Decide your set strategy before the event. Some athletes can hold large sets. Others perform better with planned breaks every 10 or 15 reps. It depends on your squat endurance, shoulder stamina, and how hard you raced the stations before it. The key is to take short, intentional breaks, not stare at the ball wondering how you got there.

Do Not Train Stations in Isolation Every Week

Station strength matters, but Hyrox is a running race with functional work built into it. At least once a week, combine running with two to four stations. For example, run 800 meters, complete a sled push, run 800 meters, row 750 meters, then repeat a similar pattern with carries and lunges.

Keep some sessions focused. A heavy sled day does not need to become a full race simulation. A technique day for wall balls and rowing should leave room to improve. But every few weeks, test your pacing in longer mixed work. Learn which stations make you reckless on the run and which ones let you recover.

A productive week often includes one longer easy run, one interval or threshold running session, two strength-focused sessions, and one Hyrox-specific mixed workout. Recovery has a place here too. If your legs are constantly crushed, your running quality drops and your station technique gets sloppy.

Practice Race Standards, Not Gym Shortcuts

Count every rep honestly. Reach the required target. Complete the full distance. Use the proper wall-ball depth and burpee standard. Training with shortcuts may feel good in the moment, but it leaves you unprepared when a judge is standing in front of you on race day.

This is where coached sessions make a difference. At Forge8 Athletics, Thursday Hyrox training blends race-specific conditioning with hands-on coaching, so you can learn the standards, scale where needed, and build toward the real demand instead of guessing alone.

Show up with a plan, but stay flexible. A missed lift, a slow run, or a broken wall-ball set is feedback, not failure. Keep forging the engine, the skills, and the discipline to take the next station one controlled rep at a time.
