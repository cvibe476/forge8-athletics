Open Gym Versus Classes for Better Results

2026-09-14 · CrossFit Forge8 Athletics · Miramar, San Diego

You walk into a traditional gym with an hour to train, a loose plan in your notes app, and no one checking whether your deadlift setup is right. You can make progress that way, but only if you can consistently program, coach, and push yourself. That is the real decision behind open gym versus classes: not which option is tougher, but which one gives you what you need to keep showing up and moving forward.

Both can build strength, conditioning, and confidence. The better choice depends on your experience, schedule, goals, and how much direction you want when training gets hard.

Open Gym Versus Classes: Start With the Real Difference

Group classes give you a programmed workout, a coach, a start time, and other people doing the work beside you. You arrive, get briefed, warm up with intention, and train under guidance. At Forge8, every class is coached and every workout can be scaled to the person in front of the coach. You do not need to know how to write a training cycle before you walk through the door.

Open gym gives you access and autonomy. You choose the session, set the pace, and decide when to train. For unlimited members with 24-hour open-gym access, that can mean lifting before an early shift, working on skills after a class, or getting in a focused session when the rest of life finally settles down.

Neither format is automatically better. Classes offer built-in structure. Open gym offers flexibility. The right answer is often a combination of both.

Choose Classes When You Need Structure That Holds Up

For most people, especially beginners or anyone rebuilding consistency, classes are the stronger starting point. Structure removes the decisions that cause training plans to fall apart. You do not have to wonder what muscle group to hit, how hard to go, or whether your workout has enough balance to support long-term progress.

A coach also sees what you cannot always feel. Maybe your knees are caving during a squat. Maybe you are cutting range of motion because the load is too heavy. Maybe you have more in the tank but have settled into a comfortable pace. Small adjustments made in real time protect good movement and make the session more productive.

Classes are also valuable when your time is limited. A well-run group session gives you a warm-up, strength or skill work, conditioning, and a clear finish line without wasting 20 minutes deciding what to do next. For working professionals, parents, and military-adjacent residents balancing demanding schedules, that efficiency matters.

The community piece is not fluff, either. Training beside people who know your name creates accountability. On the days motivation is low, a reserved class time and familiar faces can be enough to get you in the building. Once you are there, the work gets done.

Classes Are Not Only for Advanced Athletes

CrossFit group training can look intimidating from the outside when barbells, pull-up rigs, and fast-paced workouts are involved. But a good class is not built around forcing everyone into the same weights or movements. It is built around the intended stimulus.

A newer athlete may use a lighter barbell, a box squat, ring rows, or a reduced number of reps. An experienced athlete may lift heavier, move faster, or take on a more advanced variation. Both athletes can train in the same room, work hard, and leave having completed a session that fits their current ability.

That is the difference between being challenged and being thrown in over your head. You should be pushed further, not left to figure it out alone.

Choose Open Gym When Flexibility Supports Your Plan

Open gym is powerful when you already have a clear purpose. Maybe you follow a specific strength program. Maybe you are preparing for a Hyrox race and need extra sled work, running intervals, or practice on race movements. Maybe you simply cannot make fixed class times consistently because your work schedule changes week to week.

It can also be the right setting for focused skill practice. Fifteen unhurried minutes working on double-unders, strict pull-ups, handstand balance, or barbell positions can add up. These are areas where repetition and patience matter more than racing a clock.

Experienced trainees often benefit from open gym because they understand how to manage their effort. They can look at the week as a whole, avoid piling hard sessions on top of each other, and adjust a plan when fatigue builds. They know the difference between productive discomfort and the kind of pain that calls for a change.

Still, access is not the same as direction. If your open-gym workouts become random circuits, skipped strength work, or the same familiar exercises every week, flexibility can quietly turn into stagnation. More available hours do not automatically create more progress.

The Open-Gym Question to Ask Yourself

Before choosing open gym as your primary format, ask one direct question: can you honestly tell yourself what you are training for this week?

If you can name the goal, explain the plan, and track your progress, open gym may fit well. If your answer is usually, “I will figure it out when I get there,” classes will likely give you better results. There is no prize for doing fitness alone.

The Best Approach Is Often Both

For many members, classes build the foundation and open gym fills the gaps. You might attend three coached classes each week for consistent full-body strength and conditioning, then use one open-gym session for accessory work, mobility, easy cardio, or a skill you want to improve.

That mix works because each format solves a different problem. Classes keep your training balanced and accountable. Open gym lets you work around real life and give added attention to personal goals.

A Hyrox athlete may use Thursday training to practice the demands of the race in a coached environment, then add open-gym running volume or sled technique at a manageable pace. Someone focused on general health may attend classes for most sessions and use open gym for an easy recovery bike ride after a stressful week. A lifter working toward a stronger squat may follow class programming while adding carefully planned accessory work.

The key is not doing more for the sake of more. It is making sure every session has a job. Hard training needs recovery. Strength work needs consistency. Conditioning needs enough intensity to create adaptation without turning every workout into a test.

Match the Format to Your Current Season

Your answer can change over time. A complete beginner may start with classes to learn foundational movements and establish a routine. After several months, open gym may become useful for extra practice. An experienced athlete coming off an injury may lean on coached classes for smart scaling and movement feedback. A busy professional may depend on 24-hour access during a demanding work season, then return to regular class times when life settles down.

Be honest about the season you are in, not the version of yourself you think you should be. If you need external accountability, choose it. If you need schedule freedom, build around it. If you need coaching, seek coaching. Discipline is not making the hardest choice. Discipline is choosing the setup you can sustain.

What to Look for in Either Training Option

Whether you train in a class or on your own, the standard should stay high. You need a clean, well-equipped space, programming that makes sense, and an environment where effort is respected. If you are new to functional fitness, you also need a place where questions are welcomed and scaling is treated as intelligent training, not a shortcut.

For open gym, look for enough access to make your schedule realistic and enough equipment to complete your plan without competing for every rack or machine. For classes, look for coaches who actively teach, watch movement, and offer options instead of simply counting down the clock.

Your training should make you more capable outside the gym. Stronger when you lift, steadier when you move, and more confident when life demands effort. The format is only the tool. Consistent work is what gets forged over time.

If you are unsure where to begin, take a free trial class and pay attention to how it feels to train with a plan, a coach, and people in your corner. Then build the schedule that makes showing up feel possible, even on your busiest weeks.
